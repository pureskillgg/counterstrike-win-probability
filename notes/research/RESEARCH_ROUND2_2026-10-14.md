## modern-models
**Angle a summary (agent):** Evidence on modern model families for in-play win probability is thin for our setting. No source I could open tests a modern family against tuned gradient boosting on log loss and calibration at multi-million-row, second-level CS2 data. The most useful items are: (1) a CS:GO round-sequence calibration study (Leathart & Polaczuk) that shows calibration indexed by round number helps a GRU, (2) benchmark papers (Grinsztajn 2022, RealMLP, TabM) that say trees remain strong at roughly 10K rows and that tuned MLPs are competitive from 1K to 500K rows, and (3) TabPFN-2.5, which reports wins over tuned trees up to 100K rows with win-rate metrics only.  Answers to the team's questions: - Is boosted trees or random forest still best? The benchmark evidence says trees are hard to beat at around 10K rows (Grinsztajn 2022) and remain competitive up to 500K rows (RealMLP). TabPFN-2.5 claims wins over tuned trees on TabArena up to 100K rows, but reports win rates only. Nothing I found covers 4.7M rows or log loss plus calibration, so boosting remains a defensible default. - Is something more modern better? Unproven for this task. TabM and RealMLP are credible neural candidates on mid-sized tables. TabPFN-2.5 is designed for up to 50K rows and 2K features (its report says the limit is not strict and cites 100K results), so we would need to subsample our 4.7M rows. Its vendor blog's 10M-row claim is unverified. Its license also restricts commercial or production use, per an existing Meridian note I did not re-verify. Set Transformer fits the living-player table as a set, but has no win-probability evidence. - Missing features? None of these sources tests feature sets. The Bayesian soccer work (Robberechts) shows that sport-transferred models lose accuracy late in matches, which argues for explicit time-remaining features and per-time-bin evaluation. - Judging a model by actual rounds? Leathart & Polaczuk use reliability diagrams with ten equal-frequency bins, plus NLL and ECE broken down by round number. That supports a calibration plot by round time and by alive-count state. - Dependence warning: Leathart's sequences are round-level and their split unit is not stated, so their numbers are not comparable to our 0.466 second-level log loss. ## tabular-benchmarks
**Angle a summary (agent):** No source I read benchmarks random forest, extra-trees, gradient boosting and neural nets on large tabular data with log loss as the headline metric. ## inspect-rounds
**Angle a summary (agent):** Five inspection methods fit CS2 round review, and all can run on the existing data: (1) trajectory review with annotated events, (2) nearest-counterfactual comparison, (3) slice search with multiplicity control, (4) error groups defined as exact queries that include correct rounds, and (5) invariance and directional tests. No source validates a win-probability inspection method on CS2 rounds. ggViz (CS:GO, XGBoost CT-win model) is the closest analogue, but it reports no accuracy, log loss or calibration. The protocol below is our synthesis from these sources, not a published CS2 procedure.  Protocol for CS2 rounds (held-out matches only): 1. Freeze the review set before looking. Choose held-out matches and sample rounds by rule (step 2). Log every reviewed round so the review does not drift toward failures you already noticed. 2. Surprise lists. Rank held-out rounds by (a) the largest one-second jump in predicted P(CT) with no logged event nearby, (b) the worst per-round log loss, and (c) predicted P far from the outcome at a fixed time. Review the top 30 of each, plus 30 random rounds from the middle, so typical behavior is also seen. 3. Trajectory charts with events (ggViz-style). For each round, plot P(CT) per second with markers for plant, kills, deaths, defuses and round end, plus an event table. Per-round checklist: does P move in the direction the events justify; are large moves preceded by an event; is P above 0.98 or below 0.02 while the outcome is still open; is the post-plant trajectory sensible given time since plant. 4. Counterfactual check for each flagged round. (a) Find the nearest round with the opposite outcome in standardized feature space (What-If style) and compare the two trajectories. (b) Find the smallest single-feature change that moves P across 0.5 (Wachter framing), for example one fewer CT alive or 10 s more since plant, and have an analyst judge whether that state is plausible in CS2. The What-If paper estimates about 100,000 tabular points as a laptop limit, far below our 4.7M rows, so sample for interactive use. 5. Error groups as exact queries (Errudite principle). Define each group by a query, for example planted, one CT alive vs three or more T alive, under 20 s since plant, CT full buy. For each group report log loss, binned calibration, and counts of matches and rounds, and include correctly handled rounds so every group has a baseline. 6. Slice search with control (Slice Finder). Search for slices where log loss is worse than the rest by a stated effect size, with a significance test and alpha-investing or FDR control across candidate slices. Judge significance with match-level resampling. 7. Behavioral tests (CheckList). Invariance: reorder living-player rows, change player IDs the model should not use, and rename equivalent weapon names; P should not change. Directional: adding a defuse kit, helmet or armor to a CT player should not lower P(CT); adding them to a T player should not raise it. Alive-count directions are already enforced by the monotone constraints, so these tests mostly check kit, helmet and armor. Write the expected direction before running a test, and treat a violation as a lead to investigate, not a verdict.  Correlation warning: seconds inside a round are strongly correlated, and rounds in a match share players and economy. Count independent units as matches, and as rounds where needed, not seconds. Reviewing 30 rounds is 30 independent looks at the model, not 900 seconds.
