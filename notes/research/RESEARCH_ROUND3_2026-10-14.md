## additive-interpretable
**Angle a summary (agent):** Angle (a): additive-interpretable models (EBM/GA2M, NODE-GAM, NAM-type) versus gradient boosting. Short answer: the sources I could read do not show an additive model beating tuned gradient boosting on log loss at multi-million-row scale. None of them reports log loss for these models above about 285,000 rows. The most plausible role is a calibration-competitive challenger or explanation companion, not a discrimination winner. Confidence is low to moderate.  Parity at moderate size: in a 20-dataset benchmark with tuned models (up to 142,000 rows, AUROC/RMSE), EBM's average rank (4.05) beats tuned XGBoost (4.30), but CatBoost is best (Kruschel et al. 2024). In the NODE-GAM paper, EBM is within about 1 AUC point of an untuned depth-3 XGBoost on all six classification datasets in its table, and ahead of XGBoost only on Churn.  Gap at scale: on Higgs (about 11M rows), the NODE-GAM authors report error 0.3006 for additive EBM versus 0.2328 for XGBoost; adding pairwise terms brings EBM to 0.2767, still behind. On Click (about 1M rows) they are tied. The XGBoost settings for the large runs are not stated, so the size of the gap against a properly tuned XGBoost is unknown. Higgs is interaction-heavy physics data, so its transfer to our round-state data is untested.  Calibration: a single-author 2026 preprint (Manokhin, arXiv 2605.03816) on 30 binary TabArena tasks places EBM among classifiers good on both discrimination and calibration, and XGBoost and LightGBM among strong-discrimination but poorly calibrated models. It also reports that post-hoc Venn-Abers calibration helps the boosted-tree group. This suggests a possible calibration edge for EBM out of the box. It is unverified beyond the abstract-level read.  Cost: EBM training is described as somewhat slower than boosting, with no timings in the text read. Prediction is described as light on memory and fast, which fits CPU inference. NODE-GAM is much slower to train; an unopened search snippet reports about 105 s versus about 1 s for XGBoost on a 24,508-row set. The cost of selecting pairwise interactions is not quantified in any source I could read, and the original GA2M paper (Lou et al. 2013) could not be parsed.  Monotone constraints (our XGBoost constrains alive counts): the only EBM-specific evidence is a 2021 InterpretML GitHub thread (interpret 0.2.5) saying training-time monotone constraints were not supported and recommending post-fit adjustment. Current status is unconfirmed. Hu et al. (2023) describe a route that fits monotone XGBoost on selected pairwise terms and then purifies the result into an additive-plus-pairwise form. No accuracy numbers were available from the abstract.  Not covered: monotone lattice and monotone neural networks were not searched, and I found no large-scale comparison of NAM or NBM models against boosting. The Billa et al. 2026 study (216 datasets) was read but has no gradient-boosting baseline, so it was left out. No source used match-grouped splits, so all gaps quoted here come from random-row or simpler splits and may be optimistic for our setting.  Suggested test if the team wants one: fit an EBM on the same snapshot features under a match-grouped split, and score it on log loss, ECE and reliability against the current XGBoost (log loss 0.466). The evidence suggests a log-loss deficit is likely, with unknown size, and calibration is the most likely place an additive model could add value.  Table values were extracted through summarizing fetches and should be checked against the source tables before citing.

**Angle b summary (agent):** Short answer: the evidence does not show an interpretable-by-design model beating tuned boosted trees on accuracy for tabular data of this kind. The best case is a near tie with shallow or moderate boosting on AUC, with a small loss to stronger boosting. The case for an EBM-style additive model rests on explanation fidelity and auditability, not on log loss. None of the sources reports log loss, Brier or calibration for EBM versus boosting, and none uses match-grouped splits, so the decision for our 4.7M-row setting has to come from our own head-to-head.  Benchmark and practitioner evidence (all numbers read through a fetch summary of the page text, so verify before quoting): - Nori et al. (ICML 2021, arXiv 2106.09680): on 8 public tabular datasets (5.3k to 285k rows), the paper states that non-private LightGBM beats EBM in over half of its experiments. In the table read, LightGBM wins 5 of 8 and EBM wins 3. The boosted baseline is a LightGBM variant, not tuned XGBoost, and splits are random rows. - Chang et al. (KDD 2021, arXiv 2006.06466): on 10 classification datasets, mean test AUC is 86.6 for EBM, 86.6 for depth-1 XGBoost and 86.6 for depth-3 XGBoost. Logistic regression is 85.3. So an EBM ties depth-3 boosting on average AUC, but depth 4 was not tested. The same paper shows that equally accurate GAM learners can learn different shape functions, and that sparse GAMs can miss patterns and be unfair to rare subgroups. - Agarwal et al. (NeurIPS 2021, NAM, arXiv 2004.13912): XGBoost is the best baseline on all four tabular tasks in the main table. Gaps are MIMIC-II AUC 0.844 versus NAM 0.830, Credit AUC 0.981 versus 0.980, CA Housing RMSE 0.532 versus 0.562 and FICO RMSE 3.345 versus 3.490. The XGBoost tuning is not described. - Bucker et al. (JORS 2021, arXiv 2009.13384): on the public FICO HELOC data (10,459 rows, 23 covariates, random 75/25 split), a logistic scorecard is compared with gradient boosting, XGBoost, random forests, SVM, AutoML and spline logistic regression. The complex models are only slightly better than the scorecard. AUC values appear only in figures.  Interpret EBM facts (official docs, read directly): interactions default to "3x" (pairwise terms are on by default). monotone_constraints is a fit-time option, but the docs recommend monotonize() after fitting (isotonic regression), because boosting can route a constrained effect through a correlated feature. Our XGBoost constraints on alive counts would therefore need to be re-checked after switching. Training is described as slower than comparable methods, with no numbers. Prediction is additions and lookups, so CPU inference is trivial.  What is gained: exact additive term contributions, so no TreeSHAP approximation is needed, and each feature's shape can be plotted for non-experts. What is lost: pairwise-only interactions, so higher-order effects such as alive counts times time since plant times equipment are only partly represented. Accuracy margins are small but consistent in the sources (about 0.001 to 0.015 AUC, or 4 to 6 percent RMSE, against XGBoost). A smooth shape curve is also not proof of a correct one (Chang et al.).  SHAP stability on correlated features: Aas, Jullum and Loland (Artificial Intelligence 2021) show that Kernel SHAP's independence assumption can give badly wrong attributions even for linear models with correlated inputs. Their fix is dependence-aware estimation plus grouped features. Our aggregates (alive counts, health, armor, equipment, weapon class) are strongly correlated, so feature-level SHAP shown to non-experts is exposed to this. Note this paper targets Kernel SHAP, not TreeSHAP. Bucker et al. report that iBreakDown path attributions depend on feature order and that partial dependence can mismatch individual predictions under interactions, with no formal stability test. Kumar et al. (ICML 2020) argue that Shapley-based importance has mathematical problems and fits human-centric explanation goals poorly. I did not verify from a primary source how path-dependent and interventional TreeSHAP differ under correlation. A secondary snippet suggests they split credit differently among correlated features, but treat that as unconfirmed.  Seen but not included: Janzing, Minorics and Blobaum (AISTATS 2020, arXiv 1910.13413), abstract only, on the observational versus interventional choice for removed features. arXiv 2601.00428 (2026 preprint, 216 datasets, 16 interpretable methods), abstract only; it compares interpretable methods with each other and not with boosted trees, so it does not answer the question. Laberge and Pequignot (arXiv 2209.15123), search snippet only; the correlated-feature treatment was not confirmed.  Practical implication: a head-to-head on our data is cheap. Use match-grouped splits, report log loss and per-bin calibration, turn on EBM interactions and use monotonize() for the monotone constraints. Expect at best parity on log loss. The defensible gain is that the displayed curves are exact rather than an approximation of the model. Distillation of our XGBoost into an additive model was not researched here.

**Angle b summary (agent):** Short answer: none of the sources I could open shows a sequence model beating a well-tuned snapshot model on a match-level split with a proper scoring rule. The snapshot-versus-history comparison has rarely been run as a controlled test, and the esports evidence is thin. The evidence falls into three groups.  1) Snapshot sufficiency is the common finding in calibrated win-probability work. Lock and Nettleton (NFL, 2014) use only current pre-play state plus the pre-game spread, with a 2012-season holdout. Robberechts et al. (soccer, KDD 2021) use time, score, team strength and rolling summaries of play, with calibration reported. Robberechts' state-only vs state-plus-context ablation is the closest template for our question, though its numbers are only in a figure.  2) History tests that find signal either use an i.i.d. null that ignores game state (Roebber et al. 2022, NFL win-probability streaks; Klaassen and Magnus 2001, tennis points, read only from a search snippet), or lack a snapshot-only control (Yang et al. 2016, Dota 2 five-minute windows, accuracy only; Chulajata et al. 2024, fighting-game LSTM vs Transformer, AUC only). In Chulajata, AUC rises with the observed fraction of the round. My inference is that the state at the cut point carries most of the signal.  3) Markov-state reading (my inference): history should add value when a latent variable (team or player form, coordination) is unobserved and past outcomes proxy it. It should add little once the observed state contains the economy and score. A cheap check on our data is to add lagged round outcome or lagged WP-residual features to the GBM and compare match-cluster-bootstrap log loss, then compare an MLP over a short window with the same features. This is consistent with the project's finding that match history added nothing. ## entity-set-models
**Angle a summary (agent):** Angle a (entity-set / permutation-invariant models for team-vs-team state). I found no source that compares a set or permutation-invariant network against a tuned gradient-boosting model on esports win probability with log loss, calibration and match-grouped splits, so the evidence is thin and the conclusions below are partly my inference.  1. Same-task evidence is small. Xenopoulos and Silva (CS:GO, one-second states, 14.3M rows) report that a graph attention model over players (which uses player positions) is slightly worse on log loss than an aggregate-feature baseline ('State'). Adding the graph branch to the aggregate branch gives log loss 0.4276 vs 0.4351 for State, about 1.7% lower. There is no GBM baseline, the split is a single 70/10/20 split of game states with the unit unstated, and there are no CIs or calibration. The State baseline is the only gain over aggregates I found, and it is small.  2. The gain over gradient boosting came from pairwise terms. Chen et al. (DOTA 2, draft-only, 3.06M matches) report test AUC 0.7143 for a sum-form, side-antisymmetric model, equal to a factorization machine and about 0.013 above XGBoost (0.7014). The pairwise term appears to carry the gain, not the set encoder. AUC only, pre-match picks only.  3. The LoL team-as-bag multi-instance result (Birant and Birant) reports about 94% accuracy, but it is within-season 10-fold CV with grouping unstated, has no GBM baseline, and its features (kills, deaths, gold) may be post-game totals that encode the outcome. Treat it as a warning, not evidence.  4. The Horton and Lucey football axial transformer has the strongest evaluation protocol I read (season-level temporal split, calibration error reported, 62,610 games), but it predicts in-match event counts, has no baseline of any kind, and its numbers do not transfer to win probability.  5. Side symmetry: the Bradley-Terry-Elo form (Kiraly and Qian, abstract only) and the Chen et al. form are antisymmetric in the two sides by construction, which gives the 1-p constraint directly. A GBM can get the same constraint through swap augmentation or by using side-difference features (my inference, not from the sources).  Implications (my inference): - Cheapest test first: add explicit pairwise player or role-interaction features and a swap-antisymmetric formulation to the existing GBM. A hybrid (aggregates plus a small entity branch, as in Xenopoulos and Silva) is the design with some support for complementing GBM. A full set network has no evidence of beating a tuned GBM here. - Cost: a shared per-player MLP with pooling over at most 10 entities should be cheap on CPU at inference (not measured in any source). Training on 4.7M rows is feasible on GPU. The larger cost is evaluation: match-grouped CV, paired bootstrap against tuned GBM, and calibration checks. None of the sources does all three. - ESTA (already on the skip list, not cited here) appears to be the one direct comparison of boosted trees against Deep Sets and Set Transformer on esports game states, according to a search snippet I did not open. ## hybrid-and-distill
**Angle a summary (agent):** Short answer: a hybrid is more likely to add a small, calibration-driven gain on top of a tuned GBDT than to replace it. I found no source that reports log loss and calibration for a neural hybrid versus a tuned GBDT at several million rows. The large-row hybrid evidence is almost all AUC, and no source reports training or inference cost in a form that transfers to us.  What the sources show: - Log loss and calibration at scale: MCGrad (Meta, 2025) applies a LightGBM correction on top of a base model's logits, with early stopping. On 11 public datasets (11k to 3.2M rows) it lowered average log loss from 0.414 to 0.375 and ECCE from 4.66 to 1.97. But the benchmark base model was logistic regression, not a tuned GBDT, so the gain over our baseline is unknown. Production base models were GBDTs with Platt scaling, and log loss improved for 80 to 89 percent of production models. The authors note log loss can get worse on some rows. - Deep ensemble plus XGBoost (Shwartz-Ziv and Armon, 2021): beat XGBoost tuned with 1,000 HyperOpt steps on 8 of 11 datasets (up to about 1M rows), but several margins are tiny. The fetched metric labels look wrong, since the classification values match error rates rather than log loss. Treat as directional only. - Neural weak learners in boosting (GrowNet, 2020): on Higgs with about 10M rows, AUC was 0.851 versus 0.830 for XGBoost. The XGBoost tuning protocol and the full-Higgs split are not stated in the text I read. - Neural teacher distilled into trees (Pocket Foundation Models, 2026 preprint): an XGBoost student keeps 96.5 percent of a tabular foundation model's AUC at 1.9 ms on CPU. Against tuned CatBoost the gain is +0.011 AUC on datasets with fewer than 21 features and +0.001 above that, and distillation hurts where the teacher trails CatBoost. Calibration claims seen only in a search snippet are not verified. - Production hybrids (Ling et al. 2017, Bing; He et al. 2014, Facebook): a boosted-NN ensemble gave about 0.9 percent offline AUC; trees-plus-logistic-regression gave more than 3 percent over either alone, metric unconfirmed. Both are abstract-level reads.  Implications (my inference, not from the sources): - Our 4.7M rows come from 3,700 matches, so the effective sample size is closer to the match count. The large-row results above do not transfer directly, and a second-stage model can look better on row-level log loss while overfitting match-specific patterns. Any test should use match-grouped folds and a match-cluster bootstrap. - The most plausible cheap gain is an out-of-fold (grouped by match) GBDT logit used as an offset, followed by a small monotone or temperature/isotonic correction, or a MCGrad-style residual with early stopping. A full neural residual or distillation is less likely to beat that and adds CPU cost and maintenance. A GBDT plus a small correction is trivial to run on CPU. - Cost: K extra out-of-fold fits per model, one extra stage, and more code paths that must not crash on unusual input.  Read but not listed: DeepGBM (KDD 2019) distills GBDT leaf structure into a neural net. It is the closest mechanism match to a network on GBDT leaves, but I read only its abstract and a repository README, with no numbers. Guo and Berkhahn 2016 (full text read) feed neural entity embeddings into random forests and GBDTs on a 200k-row Rossmann subsample and reduce MAPE, but the plain neural net was best in the time-ordered split (0.093 versus 0.115 for GBDT with embeddings), so tree-plus-embedding hybrids did not dominate.

**Angle b summary (agent):** Angle b (loss and training choices for probability quality) has thin direct evidence. No source I could open tests proper-scoring objectives, focal loss, or per-round weighting for gradient-boosted trees on this kind of data, so the points below are partly reasoning from the sources.  Practical takeaways: 1. Keep the binary logistic objective. Log loss is both the training objective and the evaluation metric. Focal loss and label smoothing both push predictions away from observed frequencies. The calibration gains reported for them (Mukhoti 2020 for focal loss, Muller 2019 for smoothing) are for overconfident neural networks. Niculescu-Mizil 2005 found boosted trees are already biased toward the middle, away from 0 and 1, so these tweaks should not help and would need recalibration plus a log-loss check. This is inference from combining the sources. 2. Calibrate after training, not during it. Fit Platt or isotonic on held-out, match-grouped data, and check reliability curves for the S-shape Niculescu-Mizil describe. 3. Monotone constraints: the only direct evidence (Koklev 2025, single-author preprint, credit data) reports AUC cost, not log loss. It finds near-zero cost on large datasets and about 2 to 3 percent on small datasets with heavy constraint coverage. Our effective sample is about 3,700 matches, not 4.7M rows, so measure the constraint cost in log loss with a match-cluster bootstrap. The XGBoost docs warn that hist or approx can leave no valid split candidates under constraints and suggest raising max_bin. Confirm constraints hold on the GPU training path. 4. Early stopping and tuning: choosing rounds on one match-grouped validation split overstates test performance. Cawley and Talbot (2010) show selection on a noisy criterion overfits by an amount comparable to differences between algorithms. Use nested match-grouped CV, or one untouched match-level test set used once. The abstract does not name nested CV as the remedy. 5. Grouping: Roberts et al. (2017) recommend blocked CV wherever dependence exists, which supports splitting by match rather than by second. They also warn that blocking can shrink predictor ranges and cause extrapolation error, so check per-fold match-level feature ranges.  Not covered: per-round weighting and second-level subsampling have no source in this pass, so they remain untested reasoning. Gneiting and Raftery (2007, JASA, DOI 10.1198/016214506000001437) is the reference for log loss being strictly proper, but both the publisher and the DTIC copy returned 403, so it is not listed and is unverified here. Mukhoti et al. (2020) was read at abstract level but left out because of the six-source cap; its focal-loss claims are for image and NLP networks and use a loss that is not log loss.
